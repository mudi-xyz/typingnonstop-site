Which keyboard to use for typing practice and typing tests

You do not need an expensive keyboard to pass a typing test. What matters is that your practice keyboard is comfortable, reliable and similar to the one you will use on the day. Here is how to think about the choice.

Familiarity beats features

Your fingers learn the spacing and travel of the keys you practise on. A test on a very different keyboard can cost you accuracy for the first minute or two. If you know which keyboard the test uses, practise on something similar. If you do not know, practise on a normal full-size keyboard and spend some time on a laptop keyboard too.

Laptop keyboards

  • Flat keys with short travel suit some people and tire others.
  • Many laptops have no numeric keypad, which matters for data entry and 10-key tests.
  • Posture is harder because the screen and the keys are close together, so see the ergonomics guide.

Full-size desktop keyboards

  • A full-size layout with a keypad is the safest choice for office and data entry tests.
  • Most exam and office computers use ordinary membrane keyboards, so a basic one is good practice.
  • If you buy one, pick a plain keyboard with a standard layout and comfortable key feel. Avoid unusual layouts, because tests expect the usual arrangement.

Mechanical keyboards

Mechanical keyboards have a distinct feel and sound, and many people like them. They are not required and are not proven to raise test scores. If you practise only on a very light or very loud mechanical keyboard, spend time on a standard one before a test so that the normal feel does not throw you.

What to check on any keyboard

  • Every key registers every time. Sticky or double-typing keys cost you errors.
  • Your wrists can stay level and relaxed, without bending up.
  • The layout is the standard QWERTY for your country. Layout choices are discussed in Dvorak versus QWERTY.
  • The caps lock, shift and enter keys are where you expect them, which is a common surprise on small laptops.

If the test keyboard is unfamiliar

  • Arrive a few minutes early and type a few lines in any free space to get used to the keys.
  • Start the test slightly slower than your normal pace, then settle into your rhythm.
  • Do not change your hand position or technique on the day. Use what you practised.

A simple way to compare keyboards

If you own two keyboards, or can borrow one, pick one three-minute passage and type it on each at a steady pace. Record net WPM and accuracy for each. Repeat on another day. If one keyboard is clearly better for accuracy, use it for most of your practice. If the difference is small, practise mainly on the one most like your test keyboard, and use the other for a weekly run so you stay flexible.

Small changes that help

  • Keep the keyboard flat or at a slight slope that keeps your wrists straight.
  • Leave room in front for your forearms, so you are not reaching.
  • Clean the keys occasionally. Dirt can make keys feel sticky and cost you accuracy.
  • Use the same operating-system keyboard layout in practice that the test will use.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a mechanical keyboard for typing practice?

No. A comfortable standard keyboard is enough. Mechanical keyboards are a matter of preference and are not proven to improve test scores.

Should I practise on a laptop or a desktop keyboard?

Practise on the kind of keyboard your test will use. If you do not know, use a full-size keyboard mostly and try a laptop keyboard too.

What if the exam keyboard feels different?

Type a few lines before the test if allowed, start slightly slower than usual, and keep the technique you practised.
